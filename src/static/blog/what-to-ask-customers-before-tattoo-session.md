# What Should I Ask My Customers Before a Tattoo Session?

Every tattoo artist has lived this story: the client books a "small black-and-gray piece," shows up expecting full color the size of a forearm, forgot they're on blood thinners, didn't bring a reference, and now needs to be somewhere in 90 minutes. The session that should have flowed turns into damage control.

Almost all of that is avoidable. The difference between a smooth session and a stressful one is usually decided *before* anyone picks up a machine — in the questions you ask up front.

This guide walks through exactly what to ask your customers before a tattoo session, why each question matters, and how to collect the answers without drowning in DMs. At the end you'll find a copy-paste intake template you can start using today.

## Why pre-session questions matter

Asking the right questions before a session isn't bureaucracy. It directly protects three things you care about:

- **Your time.** A clear brief means less redrawing, fewer surprises, and accurate time estimates — which means accurate pricing.
- **Your client's safety.** Medical and skin information can change how (or whether) you tattoo someone.
- **Your reputation.** Clients who feel prepared and informed leave better reviews, refer friends, and come back.

Think of intake questions as the cheapest insurance you'll ever buy against a ruined session.

## The questions, grouped by purpose

### 1. The design brief

You can't quote, prep, or draw without knowing what you're actually making.

- **What do you want tattooed?** Ask for a short description in their own words.
- **Where on the body?** Placement affects pain, healing, stencil shape, and price.
- **What size, roughly?** Ask for measurements in cm/inches, or a comparison ("about the size of a credit card").
- **Color or black-and-gray?**
- **Do you have reference images?** Request them in advance, not on the day.
- **Is this a cover-up or a reworking of an existing tattoo?** If so, ask for a clear, well-lit photo of the current piece.
- **First tattoo, or do you already have ink?** This tells you how much to explain about pain and aftercare.

### 2. Scheduling and logistics

- **How flexible is your availability?** Useful for longer pieces that may need multiple sessions.
- **Roughly how much time can you commit on the day?** Some clients don't realize a piece needs six hours.
- **Are you traveling far to reach the studio?** Relevant for guest spots and multi-session work.
- **Do you understand the deposit and cancellation policy?** State it clearly and get confirmation.

### 3. Health and safety

This is the section that most protects you and your client. Keep it factual and routine — clients respect a professional who asks.

- **Are you over 18 / can you bring valid ID?** Non-negotiable in most jurisdictions.
- **Are you pregnant or breastfeeding?**
- **Do you have any skin conditions** (eczema, psoriasis, keloid scarring) **in or near the area?**
- **Are you taking blood thinners, Accutane, or other medication we should know about?**
- **Do you have allergies** — especially to latex, certain inks, or topical anesthetics?
- **Any heart conditions, diabetes, epilepsy, or fainting history?**
- **Have you used alcohol or recreational drugs in the last 24 hours?** Both affect bleeding and consent.

> A short medical questionnaire, reviewed before the appointment, is far better than discovering a contraindication when the client is already in the chair.

### 4. Expectations and comfort

- **What's your pain tolerance, and have you eaten beforehand?** A gentle reminder to eat and hydrate prevents most fainting.
- **Do you want breaks built into the session?**
- **Is there anything that would make you more comfortable** — music, a support person, a particular time of day?

### 5. The practical confirmations

- **Confirmed date, time, and studio address.**
- **Deposit paid?**
- **Aftercare instructions sent?** Sending these *before* the session means the client arrives already prepared.

## How to actually collect this — without the chaos

Knowing the questions is the easy part. The hard part is collecting the answers across Instagram DMs, emails, text messages, and the occasional voice note — then finding them again when the client walks in.

Most artists end up with information scattered everywhere:

- The design brief is in an Instagram message thread.
- The reference images are in their camera roll.
- The medical info was mentioned verbally and never written down.
- The deposit confirmation is in their email.

When intake is scattered, you spend your evenings chasing people for the same details, re-explaining your policies, and re-typing aftercare. That's administrative time stolen from the work that actually pays — and from your life outside the studio.

A better approach is to send **one structured intake form** when the client books, so every answer lands in the same place, in a format you can scan in ten seconds before the session.

Copy this, adapt it to your studio, and send it to every client when they book:

```
- Phone / preferred contact:

THE TATTOO
- Describe the design:
- Placement on body:
- Approximate size (cm/in):
- Color or black-and-gray:
- Is this a cover-up? (yes/no — attach photo if yes):
- Reference images attached? (yes/no):
- Is this your first tattoo? (yes/no):

HEALTH & SAFETY
- Are you pregnant or breastfeeding? (yes/no):
- Any skin conditions near the area? (eczema, psoriasis, keloids):
- Any medications (blood thinners, Accutane, etc.):
- Any allergies (latex, inks, anesthetics):
- Heart condition, diabetes, epilepsy, or fainting history? (please specify):

ON THE DAY
- How much time can you commit?:
- Any comfort needs (breaks, music, support person):

CONFIRMATIONS
- I've read the deposit & cancellation policy (yes/no):
- I'll eat and hydrate before my session (yes/no):
```

Keep it short enough that clients actually finish it, but complete enough that nothing important reaches the chair as a surprise.

## The bottom line

The best tattoo sessions are won before they begin. A clear design brief saves you from redrawing. A medical questionnaire protects everyone in the room. A confirmed policy prevents awkward conversations about deposits. And sending it all in one structured form means the answers are waiting for you, organized, instead of buried in a dozen message threads.

If you've ever finished a long day of tattooing only to spend your evening answering the same booking questions over and over, the problem usually isn't *what* you ask — it's *how* you collect it.
